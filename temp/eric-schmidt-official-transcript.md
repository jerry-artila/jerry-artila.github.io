## 2. Why China Has Nearly Caught Up in AI `[01:42]`

**Bob Safian:** Yes. So I want to say that I get whipsawed sometimes about the saber-rattling and jingoism that might be happening around China's advancement in AI, and I'm glad you can help demystify this for us. You know, the US had a clear first-mover advantage in AI. There's questions about whether that's going to persist or not. You've said the AI gap between the US and China has narrowed to roughly six months. What's changed most recently?

**Eric Schmidt:** The biggest change in the last year that's notable for everyone here is that the cost of software has gone from very expensive to almost nothing. And when something falls precipitously in price, you get an awful lot more of it. So when I think about the next few years, all I think about is an enormous amount of software—and what will it do, who will do it, how will it work together, how will agents work, and so forth.

China thinks the same way. And my own view is that the China-US competition is the defining competition of your generation, my generation, of our age. And the reason is that both are trying very much to develop reasoning and intelligence systems that are superhuman. And that doesn't mean like us. It means that they have superhuman or super-intelligence capabilities that are beyond human capabilities. Already, when you look at the Waymos that are running outside, they all have better vision than human drivers, especially because they don't drink—you know, you sort of get the idea, *[laughter]* right?

So, in the race, I was part of the group that put the restrictions on China some years ago on chips, and I assumed that they would work for about 10 years. A year ago or two years ago, it looked like China was a year or two behind. Now, it looks like they're six months behind. And there are many, many studies which show this. And how have they done this? After all, didn't we restrict their access to chips? We did, but they're so smart. They figured out a way to do the kind of algorithms that we develop and knowledge that we develop with essentially one-tenth of the energy and some factors less of performance.

Now, there's a huge debate in the industry as to whether that gap will get smaller or bigger, but at the moment, it's fair to say that it's a two-horse race. And that to me is the defining one. The race for intelligence is the race literally of a millennium, because once these intelligences exist, our children, grandchildren, great-grandchildren, and so forth and so on, will all be affected in really fundamental ways by the things that are occurring right here, right now in the Bay Area.

**Bob Safian:** And just to refer to some of the things we've talked about earlier here: some people say that pointing to China's AI advances is sort of an excuse and a rationale to worry about regulating later. Is there any truth to that? Do you worry about that?

**Eric Schmidt:** People make up all sorts of stuff. *[laughter]* And you know, the question—so I've been heavily involved with the China-US discussions. Dr. Kissinger and I set them up before he died, in his last visit to see President Xi, which I did with him. And the Chinese understand what the race is about. They understand the dangers of AI. And they're not stopping.

So, one way to answer your question is to say: how would a slowdown work? Okay. So, how many frontier labs are there in America? Five, six, seven—some number like that. How many are there in China? Five, six, seven. So you have a 12-body problem. On the Chinese side, presumably the government can order them to do what they want. In America, you'd have to have some kind of voluntary agreement. So now it's a two-body problem. How are we going to convince the Chinese to slow down given they already think that they're behind? Not happening. We have to come up with other ways to ensure that this technology is safe while advancing it ahead of them.

## 3. What the U.S. Can Learn from China `[05:52]`

**Bob Safian:** I mean, China has, in some ways, a structural advantage because the government can dictate AI as a national strategy and everyone sort of can align behind it. America's advantage, you've kind of argued, is sort of diversity and decentralization. These are very different approaches. Are there things that China's AI community has done that American leaders could learn from?

**Eric Schmidt:** Well, I'll give you a simple framing. China has infinite energy, most of it renewable—something we said we were going to do in our country 10 years ago and we failed to do. They don't have interconnection queue waits that go on seven years. They don't have the kind of debates. They're building the data centers, and they're building them massively.

Furthermore, even with a much more restricted financial market, the Chinese have been able to fund the startups. DeepSeek is now seeking funding. It's illegal for us to invest in DeepSeek—I know 'cause I tried. *[laughter]* So trust me, it's illegal under US law, I might add. So DeepSeek just did a huge raise. The funding looks like it's going to show up. Now, in China, you never know if it's really the government or really the private sector. My friends in China say it's the private sector, that the economics are going to work. There's a complex question involving open source, which we can talk about. But fundamentally, they're on their way. They just don't have the chips.

In the United States, we've got everything we need. We've got the smart people. We've got all the controversy. We've got incredibly deep funding markets. Where in the world could you get $50 billion on a text message? I mean, it just doesn't exist, except thank God that the American financial industry is financing our hobbies here. We'll see.

What we don't have is the energy, and we don't have the data centers. And the correct answer for this is: build your own power systems. One of the great things about America—there's an old quote from de Tocqueville about America, this is 250 years ago: “America is a great country because of the optimism of its people, the abundance of the land, and the absence of a king.” 250 years ago. And at least you can score the three. We've got a lot of land—*[laughter]*—so lots of empty land. Put these data centers in the empty land—it's good for the local economy and so forth—and build the power plants yourself. And that's what we have to do.

Current estimate is we need somewhere between 60 and 80 gigawatts of power to get through 2030, 2031. We'll see if we can get that financed. One gigawatt is about $50 billion in cost. By the way, one gigawatt is about one nuclear power plant. The last time I checked, we weren't building them at a very high rate.

## 4. How Open Models Reshape the AI Race `[08:45]`

**Bob Safian:** Yeah. You mentioned open source. China's leadership in open-source models or open-weight models—it's fueled the reach of its AI globally. For the business leaders in this room, does it make sense for them to build on Chinese open-source models or not?

**Eric Schmidt:** So let's define terms a little bit. There's something called open source. I was part of the beginning of the open-source movement back when I was at Berkeley, and very proud of that. It was a long time ago, and my code is still open source and still available for you if you want to look at very old code. And then there's something called open weights, where you spend all this money on training, and then you take the numbers—of which there are many, right, trillions of numbers—and then you make those available.

China's strategy is largely open-source and open-weights. And America's strategy is largely closed-source, closed-weights. On the American side, it makes perfect sense: these things cost $100 million to $500 million for one model.

**Bob Safian:** Right. Why just give that away if you're going to spend that money?

**Eric Schmidt:** Well, this is what the lawyers say. Now, I spent 50 years arguing with lawyers that you should give things away because it creates a platform. So the open-source movement basically is: create a platform that people build on top of. The closed-source movement, exemplified by Microsoft and basically Bill and Steve way back when—closed source: focus on the end user, do a good job or not, and so forth, and that was the test. Open source has largely won in America as the foundation for what we work on. But the large models are largely closed for good reasons. I'm not criticizing them.

There are a couple of examples. One is called Reflection AI that's just released a 500-billion-parameter model. Nemotron, which is the Nvidia product, has a similar-size model—they're both very powerful. Google has something called Gemma 4, which is also a very powerful smaller model. You can download these things. So it's not completely China versus US.

China chose open-source, open-weight because it was the most reasonable strategy for them. They were behind. They needed to get users quickly, and they're willing to take the thieving risk, if you will, and it looks like it's working because at least they're getting funded. So the contest of open-source, open-weights versus closed-source, closed-weights is the defining question. If you talk to the people here in San Francisco who are in closed-source, closed-weight companies, they'll all explain to you that their strategy is better because they'll stay ahead. If you talk to the Chinese people, they'll say their strategy is better because it's going to stay ahead. So we have a real competition here.

There are some issues, and I want to highlight one that I believe is today unsolvable, and we've spent a lot of time working on it. If you think about it, the closed-source weights have the property that it's controlled by a company. The company is controlled by the laws of that country. The CEO is subject to the law. In China, they'll just arrest you, you know, whatever. So there's real control over what those labs are doing. If you release an open-weights, open-source model such as the Chinese models, you put what are called guardrails in it, and there's a technique called “obliteration” which allows you to basically remove those restraints and then you get the raw model.

This is a problem because those raw models can be used for cyberattacks. And remember what I said at the beginning: the cost of software coming to zero. The rate at which software capabilities are expanding in the next few months is so extraordinary that the technical people here in San Francisco and elsewhere are really worried. This is the source of this “Hey, can we slow down?” kind of optimism, which is very difficult to do. So I don't fully understand how we're going to navigate through this open-source, open-weight stuff when it gets into the hands of terrorists.

You can be sure China does not want this stuff to be misused against themselves. After all, they're a one-party state, and if they're going to attack the US, they won't do it through that—they'll do it through their own active cyber war. And the same thing, US versus China, although I'm not suggesting that we should do that. This is a hard problem, and many people believe that there will be some kind of a cyber event in the next 12 months that will illustrate this point.

**Bob Safian:** And if as a business leader I decide, “Oh, I'm going to try to use that Chinese model because it's cheaper for me,” is that unpatriotic? Is that shortsighted?

**Eric Schmidt:** Well, I think American CEOs are patriotic to a point. They're also very focused on money.

**Bob Safian:** Yes, they are.

**Eric Schmidt:** Welcome to capitalism. And there are many examples where people are using small servers with open-source weights to solve some problem because the token cost is lower. The token costs are about three to five times cheaper if you run your own, and so forth. They're not as functional.

So one model for what's going to happen—which worries me, right—is that the frontier models will dominate the things that we care about here: these incredible achievements in math and chemistry and so forth. I mean, you know, we have Armageddon for mathematicians—it was like all of a sudden their whole world, which has not changed in 50 years since Gödel's hypothesis, basically everything was overthrown in one month, right? They have an existential crisis that's going to occur because of the frontier models.

For most of us, we don't need that kind of math to solve the problem of financial accounting. It just isn't that complicated, trust me. And so the idea is that many people will use these cheaper open-weight models for little things. Is that okay? It's likely to be legal in the US. Is it a good idea? Depends on what your view of Chinese threats and espionage threats and so forth and so on is. In China, of course, they'll use the open-weight models.

## 6. Why the AI Race Is Not a Zero-Sum Contest `[20:41]`

**Bob Safian:** So by our scorecard, at least in this rough way—she awarded one of them to the US, so they're tied. I guess I want to ask you: are we missing anything? Is there any scenario where the US and China both win like that, or is it a zero-sum game?

**Eric Schmidt:** I don't think AI is a zero-sum game. China's not our enemy. And I've worked in markets where two companies, although they are competitors, fundamentally create a whole new market. The best possible scenario—which of course is not possible with the current administration—sorry—so you'd have some rational conversation between the US elites and the Chinese elites saying: “How are we going to manage this? Let's agree to some things.”

So, for example, a “no surprise” rule. In my Pentagon work, I learned that when you launch a missile, you notify all the other countries and they train their satellites on your missile launch, which has two reasons: one, they make sure that you did what you said; and second, they get to train their missile-launch watchers. It's an example of an accommodation to keep everyone safe. You don't want random missile launches and not know where they're going. Makes perfect sense. These decisions were made 50 years ago. So there's a list that I think is quite rational for collaboration in order to achieve human flourishing.

And let me finish by saying that AI is the single most important thing that's going to happen for the rest of our lives. AI is the underpinning of scientific progress, which is what I'm working on. That scientific progress creates wealth. Wealth creates opportunity, blah blah blah, well-being. And for society, the future for our children and grandchildren is very, very bright because of what we're doing in AI. Yes, we've got problems. I think it's important that we continue focusing very hard on this. The innovations are coming faster than even I can keep up, and I've been doing this a long time. And second, I want it to be America.
